01 — Abstract

On Cognition, Culture, and Construction

Abstract

My practice starts from a simple conviction: nothing exists in isolation. Systems, people, ideas — everything is in constant conversation. The lenses you bring determine which conversations you can hear, and which you can start. I design at the intersection of cognitive & linguistic science, media arts & sciences, and education — three disciplines that share a single question: what creates meaning, and what does meaning create?

The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis[1] — in its weak, linguistic relativity formulation — proposes that the language available to you shapes how you perceive and categorize the world. Critically, this is not a deterministic claim. As you acquire new words, new languages, new conceptual frameworks, your perceptual space genuinely expands. The Russian distinction between siniy (dark blue) and goluboy (light blue) as categorically separate hues[2] is the clearest empirical case: Russian speakers don't merely name the boundary differently — they perceive it faster. Language didn't constrain them; it sharpened them. I apply the same logic to design. The conceptual tools I bring to a problem determine what I can see in it — and acquiring new tools, new materials, new disciplines keeps expanding what's visible.

Culture and design are mutually constitutive. Culture shapes the meanings available to us; the meanings we construct reshape culture in turn. Design sits precisely at that junction — whatever we make goes on to shape the people who use it, which shapes what they make next. I don't ask only "what does the user want?" but also "what do we become when this exists?"

My background in cognitive and linguistic science was heavily focused on language acquisition and development — the earliest stages where language and thought are most visibly co-constructing each other. That lens informs how I approach information architecture, learning design, and communication across all ages. I'm also a working designer in the physical world: painting, sculpture, woodwork, and 3D printing are not separate from my practice but continuous with it. Making in different materials — with different resistances, different feedback loops, different failure modes — teaches you things about design that screens don't.

Grounded further in the structural connectivity patterns documented in the Human Connectome Project,[3] I treat designed artifacts not as discrete objects but as nodes in broader cognitive, cultural, and social networks — asking always how information moves between minds, and what it becomes in the passage.

05 — About

About

I design products that move information between minds cleanly. My practice starts from the belief that a designer's job is partly terminological: name a problem well and you constrain the solution space productively.

I came to product design through cognitive science, where I spent three years studying how syntactic complexity affects reading comprehension — a question that translates directly to information hierarchy and interface legibility. My media arts background means I prototype in code before handing off to engineers, which has saved more than a few miscommunications.

My academic work in cognitive science was focused on language acquisition and development — how children and adults build conceptual systems through exposure, interaction, and feedback. That lens shapes how I think about information architecture and communication design across all ages, not only children. Alongside my design practice I'm working toward pre-K teaching certification, which keeps me grounded in the earliest stages of learning even as most of my professional work is with and for adults. I'm also a designer in the physical world — painting, sculpture, woodwork, and 3D printing are part of how I think, not separate from it. I'm open to collaborations in education technology, language tools, early childhood, and health UX. If you're working at any of those intersections, I'd like to hear from you.

Outside the screen: I cook with whatever's already there. The constraint is the point — a good dish, like a good design, doesn't need more ingredients, it needs more attention to the ones it has. The recipe is always a starting point, a hypothesis to adjust rather than a rule to follow. I cook mostly Latin American and Western European, though the technique moves wherever it earns its place.

I've found that the most reliable way to expand your palate is to add one unfamiliar thing at a time, introduced into something familiar — slowly enough that you can actually taste what changed. You discover, gradually, just how broad your tastes have become. Which, not incidentally, is also how you teach a toddler to eat.
